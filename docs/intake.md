# What I need before I can write the campaign

Answer what you can. "I don't know" and "I'd rather not say" are real answers —
they just change what the page can claim. Anything left blank stays out of the
copy entirely; I won't fill a gap with something plausible.

---

## 1. The ask

- **How much, and for what exactly?**
  (Retainer? Filing fees? Evaluator? Supervised visitation? Travel? Housing,
  because a judge wants to see a stable address? Back child support?)
- **Who receives the money** — you, or paid direct to a firm or the court?
- **What happens if it isn't raised?** The stakes donors are actually buying
  down. This is the single most persuasive line on any campaign page.
- **What have you already paid yourself?** Skin in the game matters to donors.

## 2. Where things actually stand

- Is there an **open case**? Court, county, roughly what stage?
- **Current order** — who has custody now, what's your access?
- **Next date on the calendar.** A deadline is what converts a reader
  into a donor.
- **Do you have a lawyer?** If not, is the money to get one?
- Has anything been **adjudicated** — findings, a protective order, a
  criminal matter? I need to know so the page never contradicts a record.

## 3. Your kids

- How many, ages, and **how long since you've seen them?**
- What is the **outcome you're asking for** — reunification, more parenting
  time, supervised to unsupervised, relocation?
- **Do you want them named or photographed?** My strong recommendation is no
  on both. They'll be able to read this page for the rest of their lives, and
  their classmates will find it.

## 4. The honest part

You said you want to account for what you did. That can live on the page —
but as a short, plain, forward-facing paragraph, not a confession.

The version that works: **what you did, what you're doing about it, what's
changed, and who can vouch.**

- What are you **actually in treatment or a program for**? Name it. Dates.
- Is there a **sponsor, counselor, pastor, case worker** who'd put their name
  to a sentence? A third party saying "he's done the work" is worth more than
  a thousand words of your own self-criticism.
- What's **concretely different now** than a year ago — job, housing, sobriety,
  anger management, completed classes?

The reason to write it this way isn't to look good. It's that "I was awful and
I'm sorry" gives a donor nothing to act on, and "I completed a 26-week program
in March, my counselor will confirm it, and I need $4,500 for a retainer" does.

## 5. Practical

- **Platform** — GoFundMe is the default for legal costs and allows custody
  campaigns. GiveSendGo and Fundly are alternatives. All require your real
  legal identity and a bank account; none allow anonymous beneficiaries.
- **Your real name is required.** There's no pseudonymous fundraising.
- **Who's sharing it?** A campaign with no network raises nothing. Realistically,
  how many people will post it?

---

## The one rule for the copy

Everything on the page has to be true and, where it matters, provable.

Not a moral point — a practical one. Misrepresentation on a fundraiser is
grounds for the platform to freeze the funds and refund donors, and in a
custody matter every sentence is discoverable. A page that overstates by 10%
is worse than no page, because it hands the other side a document where you
are on the record.

If a claim can't survive a hostile reading by opposing counsel, it doesn't
go in.

---

## The shape I'd write once you answer

1. **One sentence of who and what.** Name, kids, what you're raising for.
2. **Where it stands** — current order, next court date, the specific obstacle.
3. **What the money does** — itemized, with the total.
4. **The accountability paragraph** — short, plain, forward-facing, with the
   third party who can vouch.
5. **What changes if this works** — the outcome, concretely.
6. **What happens to leftover or unused funds.** State it. Donors look for it.

Roughly 400 words. Long campaign pages don't get read.
